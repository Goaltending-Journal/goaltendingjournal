Representative practice: reps that look like games

The key ideaA goalie learns from the information a drill gives, not from the number of pucks. Keep the real blade, the real pass option and the real release, then turn the dial on purpose.

At a glance

What the research supports, kept apart from what it doesn't

The research supports

  • Goalkeepers look at different things when they must really make the save.
  • Expert hockey goalies anchor their eyes on the puck and stick blade.
  • Hockey practice shots differ from game shots in ways that matter.

It doesn't show

  • That more game-like practice is always better. It isn't.
  • How game-like goalie reps in team practice actually are.
  • A validated way to score a goalie drill. None exists yet.

01

What "representative" means

Representative practice keeps the information and movement demands of games.[1] It has two tests.[2]

  • Functionality: does the goalie use the same information as in games? A real blade, a real release, a real pass option.
  • Action fidelity: does the goalie move as in games? A game-like stance, game-like time pressure, real pushes and recoveries.

A 2-on-1 where the carrier can genuinely choose pass or shot passes both tests. A line of shooters who always shoot from the same spot passes neither. Adaptation

02

What goalies look at

The strongest case for representative practice comes from where goalies look.

  • The task changes the gaze. Soccer keepers watching penalties on video fixated longer on the kicker's body. When they actually tried to save, they split their gaze roughly equally between kicker and ball.[3] Borrowed
  • Hockey experts watch the puck and stick. Saves came with an earlier and longer final fixation (952 vs 826 ms), placed on the puck or stick in 70.5% of trials and on the shooter's body in 2.1%.[4] Emerging
  • Late looks cost goals on deflections. When the final fixation started late, goalies conceded 41% of deflected shots against 22% when it started early.[5] Emerging
  • Elites read the blade. In a 2026 study, elite goalies fixated on the stick blade with fewer, longer looks than amateurs. Gaze did not separate saves from goals, and the authors suggest hiding shot direction in advanced practice.[6] Emerging

The useful window is short. In one lab study of slap shots from 14.6 m, cutting vision at stick-on-puck contact made no difference, so the information that matters seems to end just after the stick hits the puck.[7] A coach feeding pucks from a standstill gives very different timing information from a shooter who skates, handles and releases.

03

Volume isn't information

Hockey practice is built for skaters. The federation small-area games study filmed 24 goalies alongside 127 skaters, but analysed no goalie outcomes.[9] The first systematic look at representativeness in hockey training found that drills assumed to mimic games often didn't, and that practice shots differed from game shots on key features.[8]

A line-shooting drill with no defender, no pass option and no pre-shot movement gives a goalie lots of pucks and the wrong information. The audit below helps you see the gap in your own drills.

04

A dial, not a virtue

More game-like is not always better. In a tennis serving study, more representative practice pushed players toward placement and less representative practice toward speed. The authors concluded representativeness should be tailored to the athlete, not maximised by default.[10] Otte's PoST framework deliberately lowers it before games to build stability and confidence.[1]

So a low audit score is not a failure. It is a question: did you choose that setting on purpose, for this goalie, on this day? Module 4 gives you a way to plan the dial across a week.

05

Try it this week

Audit your most-used drill. Change the one feature the audit suggests and keep the rest. Adaptation

  • What did the goalie look at before and after the change? Ask them: "what told you where the shot was going?"
  • Did their success rate drop at first? That is normal when the information gets harder.
  • Was the drill still useful at a lower score on game day?
