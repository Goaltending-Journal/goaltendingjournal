Box control under fatigue

02

What the research says

A 2026 motion-capture study tracked the glove of 14 experienced goalies as they moved between four puck locations on synthetic ice. Each goalie's glove position was compared with their own set stance.[1]

  • Wide pucks cost the most. Glove errors were largest for pucks near the face-off dots. Goalies lost depth and drifted off angle. Emerging
  • Squareness slips first. Of the three rotation errors, the glove most often turned in or out instead of facing the puck. Emerging
  • Goalies retreat as reps pile up. Across six repeated movement patterns, the glove sank back toward the goal line. By the third rep it was measurably deeper than on the first. Emerging
  • Tired goalies are less precise. Higher self-rated exertion went with more position error (r = 0.55, a moderate link). Emerging

03

How sure we are

This is one small lab study: 14 recreational goalies (9 men's league, 5 women's league), synthetic ice, no live shots or passes, and no rink markings to orient by. It assumes each goalie's set stance is their ideal. It is the first study to measure glove box control at all, so treat it as a strong hint, not a rule. A larger on-ice study with shooters and elite goalies would tell us whether the same drift happens at higher levels.

04

On the ice

The "box" is how big the net looks from the puck. The closer a goalie plays to the shooter, the less net they need to cover. Every centimetre a goalie drifts back hands the shooter more net, and from a wide angle the margin is already small. The study suggests drift is gradual and the goalie doesn't notice it, which fits what coaches see late in penalty kills and long defensive-zone shifts.

05

In your practice plan

These are coaching applications of the findings. The study did not test them.

  1. Score the last rep, not the first. In any multi-puck sequence, judge depth and squareness on the final rep. That's where the study found the errors.
  2. Make wide pucks the finish, not the start. End sequences with a shot from the dot or the top of the circle, so the hardest angle arrives when the goalie is most tired.
  3. Build sequences that mirror real shift lengths. Six movement patterns in a row was enough to produce drift. Match drill length to a long defensive-zone shift instead of single-shot reps.
  4. Add a quick effort check. Ask for a 0-10 effort rating after each set. When ratings climb, cut the set or watch depth closely, since exertion tracked with error.
  5. Use landmarks for self-correction. Have goalies find a reference such as the dots, the hash marks or the crease edge on recovery. Lab goalies had none, and drift may be smaller when they do. That's a question worth testing.
06

What we still don't know

  • Whether elite goalies drift the same way.
  • How much drift actually changes goals against.
  • Whether blocker side behaves differently.
  • Whether any training method reduces drift.

Glove and positioning biomechanics is one of the thinnest areas in goalie research.
