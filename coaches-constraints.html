Constraints: individual, task, environment

The key ideaWhen a goalie keeps making the same mistake, change the drill instead of adding a cue. The individual, the task and the environment are the three levers.

At a glance

What the research supports, kept apart from what it doesn't

The research supports

  • Changing constraints reliably changes what athletes do.
  • Constraints come in three kinds: individual, task and environment.
  • Instructions and feedback can be planned like any other constraint.

It doesn't show

  • That changed behaviour turns into lasting learning.
  • Which constraint works best for which goalie problem.
  • Any of the goalie examples below. They are untested.

01

Newell's three kinds

Newell's 1986 model sorts everything that shapes a movement into three kinds of constraint. The coach changes them so the goalie searches for, and finds, a solution that works.[1][2]

KindWhat it coversIn the crease Adaptation
IndividualThe athlete's structure and function: height, strength, mobility, motivation, attentionHip mobility, size, fatigue, fear of high shots
TaskRules, equipment, goals and spaceShot-location rules, number of attackers, "rebound stays live"
EnvironmentThe physical and social settingRink size, lighting, crowd, team culture

A 2020 meta-analysis found that changing constraints reliably changes behaviour; for example, larger playing areas raise movement variability. It did not test whether that adds up to better learning than technique-first coaching.[4] Try the constraint explorer on the Coaches page to see each kind on a rink.

02

Change the problem, not the cue

The habit this module asks you to change is small. When a goalie keeps making the same mistake, the usual move is another verbal cue. The constraints-led move is to change the drill so the mistake gets punished, or the better choice gets rewarded, without a word from you.

Instead of saying…Change thisKind
"Don't over-challenge"Add a live far-side passer, so over-challenging gives up the backdoor goalTask
"Read the pass"Goals off a far-side pass count double (an exaggeration constraint)Task
"Find the puck through traffic"A net-front player with a "screen must stay" ruleTask
"Hold your depth late in the drill"Six movement patterns before the final shot, ending on a wide puckTask
"Find your spot"Cones at the goalie's set depth, removed over the following sessionsEnvironment

All rows are adaptations. The last two come from the Glove Positioning Analysis brief, which found glove depth drifting back from about the third rep.[6]

03

A goalie constraint menu

The same pattern works across the common goalie themes. Every row is an Adaptation: no study has tested these on hockey goalies.

ThemeConstraint to changeBehaviour it invites
TrackingAdd a deflection player or a screen; vary the shooter's angleFinding the puck around bodies instead of waiting for a clean look
Lateral playsEast–west passes before the shot, varying speed and timingPush timing scaled to the pass, no early commitment
DepthA live pass option so over-challenging is punishedDepth matched to the carrier's speed and options
ReboundsPlay continues until the puck is controlled or clearedControlling or steering the first shot to a safe area
IndividualScale shot speed and distance to size, fatigue and confidenceSuccess rates that keep the goalie searching, not surviving
Hip loadCount butterfly and RVH reps per block; log, don't capA practice plan that respects a high-risk joint

The last row matters in hockey. In one season, 69% of 101 elite Swedish goalies reported a hip or groin problem, most of them overuse.[7] No study sets a safe number of reps, so the menu asks you to count rather than cap.

04

Make it easier without breaking it

Nonlinear pedagogy separates two ways of making a task easier.[3]

  • Simplify the whole task. Slow it down, move it closer or remove options, but keep the link between what the goalie sees and how they move. A slower live shooter who still releases a real shot is simplification.
  • Avoid cutting it into parts. Butterfly slides with no shooter remove the information the slide was meant to answer. That is decomposition.

Isolated work still has a place. If one part is clearly holding the skill back, such as edge strength limiting push speed, it can be trained on its own. The theory only objects when isolated drills replace practice with real information, not when they sit beside it.

05

Instructions are constraints too

Otte and colleagues treat instructions and feedback as "instructional constraints": something the coach designs in advance, like the size of the area or the number of shooters.[5] Talking after every rep works against a block meant to let the goalie find a solution. Module 4 turns this into a feedback plan for each stage of a session.

Keep cues pointed at the effect of the movement, such as "arrive set before the blade moves", rather than at body parts.

06

Try it this week

Pick one theme from the menu. Change one constraint in one drill and keep everything else the same, so you can see what the change did. Adaptation

  • Which constraint did you change, and which kind was it?
  • What did the goalie do differently in the first five reps? In the last five?
  • Did you give fewer cues than usual?
