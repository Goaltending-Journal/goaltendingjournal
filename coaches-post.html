Read this first: untested, and no hockey use yet
  • PoST comes from soccer goalkeeping.
  • It is an expert planning framework, not a tested method. No study in any sport has compared PoST-planned training with another approach.
  • Nobody has applied it to ice hockey. Every hockey example in this module is our adaptation.
  • Two newer goalkeeper studies by Otte's co-authors could not be read before this module was written.[8]

At a glance

Why it's still worth learning

What it gives you

  • A shared language to label every block of practice.
  • A way to see the imbalance in your own week.
  • A one-page session sheet and a feedback plan.

What it doesn't

  • Proof that PoST-planned practice makes better goalies.
  • Hockey drills. Its soccer drills mostly don't transfer.
  • Safe rep counts or ice-time targets. Nobody has set them.

01

Three stages

PoST (Periodisation of Skill Training) is built for specialist coaches working with one to four athletes. It sorts practice into three stages, with the middle one split in three.[1] The hockey column is our translation. Adaptation

StageGroupWhat it's forIn the crease
1 · Coordination1–2Stable basic patterns, keeping the link between seeing and movingT-push, shuffle, butterfly slide, RVH entry and exit, tracking to a real release
2a · Movement Variability1–2Vary one skill rep to repVary release point, handedness, shot type, elevation, pass speed and rebound direction
2b · Complexup to 4Link skills and vary between them; add fatigue and pressurePoint shot, live rebound, pass to the backdoor; two nets and two goalies running chains
2c · Team-based4 to full teamPhases of play and small-sided games, coached by questionsSmall-area games with goalie rules; 3-on-3 cross-ice; 5-on-5 scrimmage
3 · PerformanceAnyGame-like first, then stability and confidence close to the gameMorning skate and goalie-led pre-game warm-up

Movement between stages is not a ladder. When a goalie moves consistently in controlled conditions over several sessions, that is the trigger to move on. A goalie who is overwhelmed in Complex work can drop back to Movement Variability in the same session.[1]

02

Two dials

Every block sits on two dials.[1]

  • Representativeness: how closely the block copies a game (module 3).
  • Perceived complexity: how hard the block feels to this goalie. The same drill can be easy for one goalie and overwhelming for another.

Neither dial has a validated measure; the authors proposed a "rating of perceived challenge" but never built it. Score them roughly, and use the drill audit for the first one.

Otte built PoST after interviewing 15 expert goalkeeper coaches. They named decision-making as central, yet ran sessions mostly from simple to complex and prioritised isolated technique.[3] The PoST paper cites survey data in which complex, game-like work filled only about 30% of professional keeper coaches' session time, against 50–70% for isolated technique.[1] Hockey Canada's in-season pathway allocates about 85% to technical skills.[5] A curriculum isn't the same as observed practice, so that comparison is loose, but most coaches who run the audit will find the same tilt.

03

Plan your feedback

Otte's feedback paper treats instructions as a constraint you design, not a running commentary.[2] It works in three steps: identify the stage, choose a method, then set how much detail to give.

Seven methods

Instructive or direct · task-oriented · question and answer · trial and error · live or recorded video · model learning · analogy learning.

Six guidelines

  1. Design first.
  2. Match the method to the stage.
  3. Limit the volume.
  4. Delay feedback.
  5. Avoid prescribing solutions.
  6. Individualise.

A default for each stage Adaptation

StageDefault methodExample
CoordinationTask-oriented, one external cue"Arrive set before the release"
Movement VariabilityTrial and error; one question after each set"What told you the pass was coming?"
ComplexBandwidth: speak only when the same breakdown repeatsSilent until the second missed backdoor read
Team-basedQuestions at whistles; video afterwardsFilm one goalie's reps on a phone
PerformanceShort, confident, familiar cuesThe goalie's own warm-up words

The feedback model synthesises general sport psychology and pedagogy research, so it is graded Borrowed. The stage defaults and hockey analogies such as "close the door" for sealing the post on RVH are Practice wisdom.

04

What hockey adds

The planning logic transfers. The soccer drills mostly don't: varied ball sizes, vision-limiting glasses and three keepers defending three goals all fit poorly on ice. Hockey also brings constraints PoST never considered. Adaptation

  • A hip-load budget. Count butterfly and RVH reps per block. 69% of 101 elite Swedish goalies reported a hip or groin problem in one season.[6] No study sets a safe number, so log rather than cap.
  • Shooter quality as a dial. Shooter skill and instructions are a constraint you control.
  • Puck-speed safety. Surprise (screens, late releases) is riskier for young goalies; progress speed with age and equipment.
  • One goalie in team practice. Complex and Team-based work has to live inside the head coach's plan.
  • Ice costs money. Move Coordination and some variability work off the ice; spend team ice on game-like work.
  • Rebound chains. Recover-and-reset is a core hockey unit with no real soccer equivalent. NHL goalies made about 32 full butterflies and 20 exits to play the puck per game in one older study.[7]

A worked session

Goalie-only ice, two goalies, two or three shooters, 50 minutes. Mid-week theme: lateral plays and the second save. Adaptation

TimeStageTask and constraintsDialsFeedbackLog
0–8CoordinationGoalie picks a movement pattern, then tracks a slow, real release from three spots and holds a setLow / lowOne external cue at mostSet at release yes/no
8–20Movement VariabilityCross-slot pass and shot; pass speed, release delay, handedness and shot type change every repMedium / low–mediumTrial and error; a question after each set of 6Butterfly/RVH count; controlled vs dangerous rebounds
20–34ComplexPoint shot, rebound stays live, pass to the backdoor shooter. A backdoor goal counts doubleHigh / medium–highSpeak only when a breakdown repeats twiceFreeze / safe / dangerous rebound
34–44Complex → Team-based2-on-1 rush: carrier truly chooses pass or shot; a third player enters late as a screenMedium / highQuestions only; film one goalieCoach 1–5 read rating
44–50SplitThe goalie who struggled drops to a slower variability set. The other repeats last week's lateral block cold, with no feedbackSet by needNone during the retention checkRetention vs last week; session RPE

The heaviest block for the hips sits early and short, and its count is logged. The session ends with PoST's split by how each goalie is coping, and with a retention check that module 5 explains.

06

Starter and backup weeks

PoST's week puts learning stages early and Performance before the match. Hockey weeks hold more games, so the starter and backup need different profiles. This draft is for two goalies with games on Friday and Saturday. Adaptation

DayPlanWhy
SunOff / mobilityRecovery after a weekend set
MonOff-ice Coordination and puck-less patterns; video review by questioningCheap stage work moves off the ice
TueGoalie-only ice: Movement Variability, then Complex. Team practice later as goalie in drillsLearning stages early in the week
WedTeam practice: small-area games with goalie rules added (screen must stay, rebounds live)Most game-like day of the week
ThuPerformance (early): game-like scrimmage against the next opponent's tendencies, lower volumeComplexity starts to drop
FriPerformance (late): goalie-led, familiar warm-up and morning skate. GameStability and confidence
SatLight Performance; backs up or restsProfiles swap with the backup

The backup has fewer game reps, so needs more game-like work in practice. Sum each goalie's butterfly/RVH count and session effort across the week, so a heavy Tuesday isn't followed by a heavy Wednesday. No source sets a safe weekly number; learn your own goalie's tolerance.

07

Try it, film it, log it

  1. Label. Run the audit above on last week.
  2. Redesign one block. Move it one stage toward the game, or add a feedback plan, on the session sheet.
  3. Film it. A phone behind the net is enough.
  4. Check it next week. Repeat the block cold at the start of a session, with no feedback. Module 5 shows how.
