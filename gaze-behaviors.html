Gaze Behaviors of Goaltenders

The Study

Panchuk D, Vickers JN. Gaze behaviors of goaltenders under spatial–temporal constraints. Human Movement Science. 2006;25(6):733–752. doi:10.1016/j.humov.2006.07.001

Why it's a Foundational Study: this was the first on-ice eye-tracking study of hockey goalies. It introduced the "quiet eye" to goaltending, it is the most cited goalie vision paper (over 200 citations), and every goalie gaze study since has built on it or tested it.

Breakdown

Eight elite university goalies, with about 15 years of play each, wore a mobile eye tracker on the ice. Each team's best shooters took wrist shots at them from 5 and 10 metres. The study measured the "quiet eye": the goalie's final steady fixation before the puck is released.

  • Experts lock onto the puck and blade. About 71% of the quiet eye landed on the puck or stick, 26% on the ice just in front of the release point, and only 2% on the shooter's body. Emerging
  • Earlier and longer goes with saves. On saves, the quiet eye started earlier in the shot and lasted longer than on goals: about 950 ms versus 830 ms. Every one of the eight goalies showed a longer quiet eye on their saves. Emerging
  • Saves are prepared before release. The goalies started moving just 20 to 46 ms after the puck left the stick, faster than visual reaction time allows. They were reading the release, not reacting to the puck in flight. Emerging
  • Distance didn't decide it. Shooting from 5 or 10 metres changed the timing of the shot, but not whether the goalie made the save. Emerging

Takeaways

This is a small study: eight adult male university goalies facing one shot type (wrist shots) from two fixed distances, with no screens, passes or deflections, and a 2006-era eye tracker.

The pattern has held up over 20 years. A 2016 follow-up by the same group found that goalies who fixated the puck early and held it longer made more saves on unpredictable deflections (Panchuk, Vickers & Hopkins). A 2026 Finnish study found elite goalies make fewer, longer fixations and hold a longer quiet eye than amateurs (Laukkanen et al.).

That same 2026 study, however, found no gaze difference between saves and goals. So "experts look this way" is well supported, while "this gaze wins each shot" is still an open question.

For Coaches

Hockey goalies don't "read the shooter's body" the way soccer keepers read a kicker's hips. The useful information is on the blade and the puck at release, and the best goalies find it early and keep their eyes quiet.

A wrist shot from 5 metres reached the net in about a tenth of a second, which is faster than anyone can react. The save is built from what the goalie sees before the puck leaves the stick. An early, stable read sets up the save before the puck changes direction.

Practice Pointers

These are suggested coaching applications from the GRJ. These are not associated with the study in any way.

  1. Coach "find the blade early, then stay still" rather than "watch the puck into your body." The quiet eye is about when the eyes settle and how steady they stay.
  2. Use live shooters who hide their intent. Ask them to vary the release point and avoid telegraphing. Predictable coach-fed shots let goalies guess instead of read.
  3. Add redirect stations. Tips and deflections reward an early, steady fixation on the release.
  4. Practice pass-or-shoot reads. 2-on-0 and 2-on-1 reps where the puck carrier decides late build the habit where it's hardest.
  5. Ask "What did you see at release?" after reps. You don't need an eye tracker; the goalie's answer tells you whether they named the blade and puck or the shooter's body.

Major Questions

  • Can gaze training improve on-ice saves for hockey goalies? No controlled trial exists yet.
  • Does the quiet eye predict single saves, given the 2006 and 2026 results disagree?
  • How do goalies use their eyes through screens and traffic, on lateral passes, or in games?
  • Does any of this hold for female, youth or sledge goalies? Every study so far tested adult men.
  • Perceptual training trials are one of the GRJ's open questions for the field.
